**The question:** if one gap in the plot is twice another, was it twice as big in the
data?

This is a stricter claim than the neighbourhood family makes, and a different one.
Neighbourhood measures only care about *who* is near whom; these care about *how far*.

## The measures

<i class="dir">▲</i> higher is better · <i class="dir">▼</i> lower is better · <i class="dir open">△</i><i class="dir open">▽</i> hollow: unbounded, so the number is comparable only between projections of the same data.

| measure | | what it compares | range |
| ---- | ---- | ---- | ---- |
| `stress` | <i class="dir open">▽</i> | raw distances, as they are | $[0, \infty)$ |
| `scaleNormalizedStress` | <i class="dir">▼</i> | distances after the optimal global rescaling | $[0, 1]$ |
| `nonMetricStress` | <i class="dir">▼</i> | distances after the optimal *monotone* transform | $[0, 1]$ |
| `pearsonR` | <i class="dir">▲</i> | linear correlation of the two distance sets | $[-1, 1]$ |
| `spearmanRho` | <i class="dir">▲</i> | rank correlation — ordering only | $[-1, 1]$ |
| `residualVariance` | <i class="dir">▼</i> | $1 - r^2$; the variance the linear fit leaves | $[0, 1]$ |

They form a ladder of how much you are willing to forgive:

- `stress` forgives nothing. A projection that is perfect but drawn at half scale
  scores badly.
- `scaleNormalizedStress` forgives a global rescaling: it minimises
  $\sum (d_{ij} - \alpha \hat{d}_{ij})^2$ over $\alpha$, so `optimalScale` is the factor
  the *projected* distances are multiplied by, not divided by. Usually what you want,
  and what most papers mean when they say "stress".
- `nonMetricStress` forgives *any* monotone transform — it only asks that the
  ordering survive. Equivalent in spirit to `spearmanRho`, computed as a stress.

Only raw `stress` is unbounded above. The other two each minimise their numerator
against a fit that includes the trivial one — $\alpha = 0$ for the rescaling, the
constant curve for the monotone fit — which is exactly the denominator, so neither
can exceed 1.

The shared distance pass, and `spearmanRho`, sum over the **full $N \times N$ matrix,
zero diagonal included** — zadu's convention, so the numbers line up with it. For the
stresses that changes nothing, since a diagonal term contributes zero to numerator and
denominator alike. For `pearsonR`, `residualVariance` and `spearmanRho` it changes the
answer: all three take means over the entries and the $N$ zeros shift them. Those
three will not agree with a condensed (upper-triangle) implementation on the same
data. Compare like with like. (`nonMetricStress` is the exception: it enumerates the
$i < j$ pairs only.)

## Using them

All but `spearmanRho` and `nonMetricStress` come free with the distance accumulators,
which `analyze` collects by default:

```js
const a = analyze(X, Y);

stress(a.moments).value;                 // 0.0807
scaleNormalizedStress(a.moments).value;  // 0.0737
pearsonR(a.moments).value;               // 0.9967
residualVariance(a.moments).value;       // 0.0066 — the 1 − r² of the same fit
optimalScale(a.moments);                 // the factor the projection is multiplied by
```

`spearmanRho` and `nonMetricStress` are separate calls, because both must
materialise every pair to sort them:

```js
spearmanRho(hd, ld).value;      // 0.9880
nonMetricStress(hd, ld).value;  // 0.0311
```

<Aside type="danger" title="`stress` is a share, not a score">
Its per-point array has `localKind: "share"` — the entries sum to 1, and each one is
that point's *fraction of the total error*. A large value means a large contribution,
not a bad point. Averaging it does not give the stress.
</Aside>

## Where they disagree with each other

When a projection keeps every ordering but gets the *size* of the gaps wrong — which
is what t-SNE and UMAP do by construction — the metric measures condemn it and the
rank-based ones approve:

<Disagreement client:visible id="gaps-compressed" />

Three rigid clusters pushed together until the gaps between them are a quarter of
their true size. `spearmanRho` reads 0.9993 and `nonMetricStress` 0.008, because the
clusters are still further from each other than their own points are. But
`scaleNormalizedStress` reads 0.141 — seventeen times the non-metric figure — and raw
`stress` 0.747.

Nothing is wrong with any of them: they answer different questions, and only one of
those questions is usually the one you meant.

<Aside type="tip" title="A useful ratio">
`scaleNormalizedStress` much larger than `nonMetricStress` means the projection has
the *shape* right and the *scale* wrong. The two agreeing means the ordering is broken
too, and no monotone rescaling would rescue it.
</Aside>

<Aside type="note" title="Which to quote">
If you are going to read absolute distances off the plot, `scaleNormalizedStress`.
If you are only going to read the ordering — "these two are closer than those two" —
`spearmanRho` or `nonMetricStress`, and say so.
</Aside>

## Cost

`stress`, `scaleNormalizedStress`, `pearsonR` and `residualVariance` are $O(1)$
read-outs of the pass.

`spearmanRho` and `nonMetricStress` are $O(N^2 \log N)$ *and* materialise every pair.
Both take a `maxPairs` guard, default `60e6` — but they count different things, so the
same number means different sizes.

- `spearmanRho` builds the full $N \times N$ matrix, diagonal included, and counts
  $N^2$ against `maxPairs`. It refuses past $N \approx 7\,745$, so at
  $N = 10\,000$ it throws.
- `nonMetricStress` enumerates the $N(N-1)/2$ pairs with $i < j$ and counts those. At
  $N = 10\,000$ that is $5.0 \times 10^7$ — **under** the default, so it does not
  refuse, it runs. It then holds six arrays over those pairs (both distance sets, the
  sort order, the two sorted copies and the disparities), about 44 bytes per pair in
  total, and tries to allocate over 2 GB. Lower `maxPairs` if that is not what you
  want.

## Verification

Every measure here is checked against a reference fixture, at these tolerances:
`stress` against zadu to 1e-12, `scaleNormalizedStress` to 1e-10 (and `optimalScale`
against zadu's own $\alpha$ to 1e-12), `pearsonR` to 1e-11, `residualVariance` to
1e-10, `spearmanRho` against `scipy.stats.spearmanr` — tie-correction included — to
1e-10, and `nonMetricStress` against zadu's `non_metric_stress` end to end to 1e-10.
The isotonic step is pool adjacent violators, matching
`sklearn.isotonic.IsotonicRegression`'s handling of tied inputs, but it is the
end-to-end value that is compared, not the fit in isolation.
